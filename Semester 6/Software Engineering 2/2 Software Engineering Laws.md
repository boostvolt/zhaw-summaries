# Humphrey's Law
> "People don't know what they want until they see it." 

**Explanation**: Users often cannot fully articulate their needs for a piece of software without first interacting with a version of it.

**Implication**: This is a primary reason why companies like Google, Microsoft, and Apple prefer to release versions rather than waiting for a "perfect" final product. They present something to the user, who then provides feedback, leading to iterative improvement.

**Core Idea**: It signifies a shift from attempting to deliver a perfect initial solution to a model of constant improvement. There is no perfection, only constant betterment.
# Ziv's Law
> "Software development is unpredictable and can never be fully understood."

**Explanation**: Also known as the "uncertainty principle," this law states that unpredictability is inherent in software development, much like in life, especially when doing something for the first time.

**Implication**: Most things in life (and software) are so complex that complete understanding is elusive.

**Core Idea**: It emphasizes the need for strategies to cope with inherent uncertainty.
# Conway's Law
> "Software is a mirror of the company and the people who design it."

**Explanation**: Originating in the 1960s, this law suggests that all products we create and all work we do are reflections of ourselves and our organizational structures.

**Implication**: The structure of a software system will reflect the social structure of the organization that developed it.

**Core Idea**: It prompts reflection on whether one can see themselves in their work.
# Brook's Law
> "Adding manpower to a late software project makes it later."

**Explanation**: When a project is behind schedule, adding more people can increase delays due to increased communication overhead, onboarding time, and potentially leading to a more complex and brittle product (tying into Conway's Law).
# Parkinson's Law
> "Work expands so as to fill the time available for its completion."

**Project Management Implication**: Without rough deadlines for milestones, projects risk never being completed. This underscores the importance of iterating on a Minimum Viable Product (MVP) within fixed timelines.

**Adaptation for Resources**: The law can be adjusted for data, CPU, memory, etc.: "Data/CPU/memory usage expands to use up all available storage space/bandwidth/cycles/RAM."
# Pareto's Fallacy
> "When you're 80% done, you think you only have 20% left."

**Explanation**: This is a misinterpretation of the Pareto Principle (80/20 rule). The critical overlooked part is that the remaining 20% of the work will often require 80% of the total time.

**Related to**: Iceberg Fallacy.
# Sturgeon's Revelation
> "90% of everything is crud."

**Implication**: This applies to software products as well; a vast majority of output may not be of high quality or utility.
# The Iceberg Fallacy
> "The cost of development of a new software product is the only ~25% of the total cost of ownership management sees and budgets for."

**Ops Adage Tie-in**: The joke here is: even if maintenance consumes 75% of total software costs, the ongoing operational support costs are so substantial and routinely ignored that they might as well be another 75%, a humorous jab at how deeply these expenses are underestimated.

**Related to**: Pareto's Fallacy.
# The Peter Principle
> "In a hierarchy, every employee tends to rise to their level of incompetence. Thus, in time, every post tends to be occupied by an employee who is incompetent to carry out its duties."

**Connection**: Dunning-Kruger effect is often mentioned in relation

**Dunning-Kruger effect explained**: A cognitive bias where individuals with low ability or knowledge in a specific area tend to overestimate their own competence and, conversely, often fail to recognize their lack of skill or the expertise of others.
# Eagleson's Law
> "Any code of your own that you haven't looked at for six or more months might as well have been written by someone else."

**Corollary (Yo Momma Corollary)**: Only the original author is permitted to criticize their old code; negative feedback from others is often dismissed.
# Greenspun's 10th Rule of Programming
> "Any custom developed authentication system contains an ad hoc, informally-specified, bug-ridden, slow implementation of half of Kerberos."

**Generalization (Universal NIH-Rule)**: "Any custom developed system contains an ad hoc, informally-specified, bug-ridden, slow implementation of half of the industry standard you refused to adopt." (NIH = Not Invented Here)
